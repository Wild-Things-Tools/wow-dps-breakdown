# WoW DPS Breakdown

A site that answers three questions about every World of Warcraft damage spec, from
simulation rather than opinion:

1. **How much** damage does it do, at one target through ten?
2. **Does it funnel** — does the main target take *more* damage because the other
   targets are there, or does area damage cost it?
3. **When** does it happen — one big cooldown window, or a flat line?

Everything is generated by [SimulationCraft](https://github.com/simulationcraft/simc)
on a schedule. No numbers are entered by hand, and nothing needs to be re-run manually
when a patch lands.

---

## What "funnel" means here

Funnelling is when the main target takes **more** damage *because* the other targets are
there — damage-over-time effects on adds generating resources and procs that get spent on
the priority target. The tell in play is using single-target spenders at five targets
anyway, so the boss dies first.

That is a different question from how the damage happens to be distributed, and the site
reports both because they frequently disagree:

| | Formula | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Funnel gain** | main-target DPS at N targets ÷ DPS at 1 target | Above 1.0 the adds feed the boss; below 1.0 area damage costs it. |
| **Concentration** | main-target share × N | 1.0 is an even spread, N is everything on the main target. |

Concentration alone is misleading: a spec with no area damage scores maximum
concentration while gaining nothing from the adds. At five targets, Beast Mastery Hunter
concentrates hardest of all (3.09×) but its boss damage is 0.99× what it would be alone —
it is not funnelling, it just has little area damage. Unholy Death Knight concentrates far
less (1.82×) and genuinely funnels (1.06×).

## How the measurement works

This is the part worth understanding, because it is the reason the site exists and the
reason its numbers can be trusted.

SimulationCraft has **no per-target damage breakdown** — that has been an open feature
request [since 2012](https://github.com/simulationcraft/simc/issues/1091). But it does
track one thing that turns out to be exactly what we need. In
`action_t::assess_damage`, every damage event whose target is the sim's primary target
is accumulated into a separate counter:

```cpp
else if ( state->target == sim->target || ( sim->merge_enemy_priority_dmg && … ) )
{
  player->priority_iteration_dmg += state->result_amount;
}
```

That counter surfaces in the JSON report as `prioritydps`, emitted whenever more than
one enemy is present. It is a **measured** figure for how much damage the main target
actually took — no modelling assumptions layered on top. From it:

```
funnel gain   = prioritydps(N) / dps(1)            → did the adds help the boss?
concentration = prioritydps(N) / dps(N) × N        → where did the damage go?
```

Funnel gain needs the single-target baseline, so it is computed once a spec's whole
target sweep is in hand, not from a single report.

### Real numbers this produces

At five targets, Midnight season 2 profiles, sorted by funnel gain:

| Build | Main target | Alone it would take | Funnel gain | Concentration |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Affliction Warlock (Hellcaller) | 212,022 | 199,860 | **1.06×** | 2.46× |
| Unholy Death Knight (San'layn) | 236,286 | 223,254 | **1.06×** | 1.82× |
| Beast Mastery Hunter | 228,562 | 230,892 | 0.99× | **3.09×** |
| Elemental Shaman (Farseer) | 131,513 | 241,879 | 0.54× | 1.19× |

The Hunter concentrates hardest and funnels least. Affliction and Unholy genuinely make
the boss die faster by having adds up. Elemental Shaman pays nearly half its boss damage
for its area damage. Ranking by concentration alone would get this exactly backwards.

Affliction is also the clearest case for looking at the whole curve rather than one
number: its gain peaks at 1.06× around five targets and falls to 0.70× at ten, where
spreading damage-over-time effects stops paying for itself.

### What SimulationCraft does and does not capture

It models the **mechanics** correctly: resources and procs generated by extra targets
flow through the simulation, which is what produces the gain above 1.0 in the first
place.

It does not generally model the **decision** to funnel. Default rotations maximise total
damage, and in these fight styles no target ever dies, so nothing rewards holding
single-target spenders for the boss. The one spec whose rotation reasons about this
explicitly is Enhancement Shaman, which carries `expected_lb_funnel` and
`expected_cl_funnel` variables comparing Lightning Bolt against Chain Lightning.

So read these numbers as *what funnelling costs or pays while playing the standard
rotation*, not as the funnel ceiling. Measuring the ceiling would need a second pass
optimised for priority damage — see Planned, below.

## What gets simulated

Four scenarios, chosen so each answers something different:

| Scenario | Fight style | Targets | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Patchwerk** | `Patchwerk` | swept 1 → 10 | The clean laboratory measurement. Static targets, all alive the whole fight, and the only scenario swept across target counts. |
| **Add Waves** | *(none — built here)* | boss + waves of 5 adds | What funnelling is actually about: a boss that matters throughout, adds that come and go. Its baseline is Patchwerk at one target — identical settings apart from the adds. |
| **Hectic Add Cleave** | `HecticAddCleave` | boss + spawning adds | simc's own add style. Reports the main-target share but no funnel gain: it layers movement events on the adds, so there is no add-free run with the same movement to divide by. |
| **Dungeon Slice** | `DungeonSlice` | simc's M+ approximation | Roughly a six-minute slice of a Mythic+ dungeon, averaging about four targets. |

Add Waves is built here rather than taken from a simc fight style for one reason: every
built-in add style bundles movement events, which would charge movement downtime to the
adds and make the comparison against single target meaningless. Ours is a boss plus five
adds arriving every sixty seconds and staying twenty — about a third of the fight with
adds up, and nothing else changed.

Dungeon Slice deliberately reports neither funnel gain nor concentration: in that fight
style simc counts priority damage against *bosses* rather than against the primary
target, so the figure would not mean the same thing. The site says so rather than showing
a number that looks comparable and is not.

### Hero talents come for free

simc's tier profiles encode the hero talent build in the profile name
(`MID2_Mage_Fire_Frostfire`, `MID2_Mage_Fire_Sunfury`), so Frostfire versus Sunfury Fire
Mage is one click apart on the site. When the simc developers add a spec or a new hero
build for the current tier, the next nightly run picks it up with no code change here.

The build identity is read from the profile *body*, not the filename — simc keeps the
internal name accurate when the recommended build changes but does not always rename the
file. `MID1_Death_Knight_Unholy.simc` declares itself as `..._Unholy_Rider`, and Rider is
genuinely the build being simmed.

## Repository layout

```
pipeline/          Python: profile discovery, simc orchestration, metric extraction
  src/wowdps/
    scenarios.py     fight styles, target sweeps, statistical settings
    profiles.py      finding and identifying simc tier profiles
    simc_runner.py   invoking simc, collecting json2
    parse.py         concentration, ability shares, timelines, burst ratio
    dataset.py       writing the static JSON the site reads
    warcraftlogs.py  optional cross-check against real raid logs
web/               React SPA (Vite, TypeScript, Tailwind v4, Recharts)
  public/data/       the generated dataset, committed
.github/workflows/
  ci.yml             lint, typecheck, test on every push
  sims.yml           nightly simulation matrix → commits fresh data
  deploy.yml         builds and publishes to GitHub Pages
  logs-verification.yml   optional weekly Warcraft Logs comparison
scripts/build-simc.sh     local SimulationCraft build
```

## Running it locally

Build SimulationCraft once (takes a while the first time — it is a large C++ project):

```bash
./scripts/build-simc.sh
```

Install the pipeline and see what would be simulated:

```bash
pip install -e './pipeline[dev]'
wowdps list --profiles .work/simc/profiles
```

Run a small dataset — a couple of builds at low precision, for a fast loop:

```bash
wowdps build \
  --simc .work/simc/build/simc \
  --profiles .work/simc/profiles \
  --spec mage_fire_frostfire \
  --spec shaman_elemental_stormbringer \
  --target-error 1.0 \
  --out web/public/data
```

Then run the site:

```bash
cd web
npm install
npm run dev
```

A full run at publication quality is `--target-error 0.2` across every profile: roughly
a thousand simulations. That is why CI splits it across parallel shards.

### Useful flags

| Flag | What it does |
|---|---|
| `--target-error 0.2` | Converge until the DPS standard error is below this percent. `0` switches to a fixed, reproducible iteration count instead. |
| `--shard 0/6` | Run one slice of the matrix. Round-robin, so shards stay balanced. |
| `--scenario patchwerk` | Limit to one scenario (repeatable). |
| `--wow-class Mage` | Limit to one class (repeatable). |
| `--tier MID2` | Pin a tier directory instead of using the newest non-empty one. |
| `--include-tanks` | Also simulate tank specs, which are excluded by default. |

## How it stays current without anyone touching it

- **Nightly** (`sims.yml`): clones the latest SimulationCraft, builds it with a cached
  build tree, splits the profile matrix across parallel jobs, merges the results and
  commits the dataset. A balance patch or an APL fix is on the site the next morning.
- **On every push to `main`** (`deploy.yml`): rebuilds the SPA and publishes it into a
  separate **public** hub repository — including the data commits the nightly job makes.
  The source repository stays private; only the compiled site travels. This needs a
  `HUB_PAGES_TOKEN` secret (a fine-grained PAT with *Contents: read and write* on the hub
  repo). If the source repo is ever made public, native GitHub Pages is simpler and the
  workflow can be pointed back at it.
- **Weekly, optional** (`logs-verification.yml`): pulls ranking medians from Warcraft
  Logs for a reality check. Skips itself cleanly if credentials are not configured.

The nightly job publishes whatever finished. One spec that fails to converge costs that
spec's cell, not the whole day's data, and the failure is recorded in the spec's own file
so the site can show it.

### Enabling the Warcraft Logs cross-check

Register a client at <https://www.warcraftlogs.com/api/clients/> and add
`WCL_CLIENT_ID` and `WCL_CLIENT_SECRET` as repository secrets. The public API allows
3,600 points per hour, which is far more than a weekly pass over a raid tier needs.

Logs and sims disagree for real reasons — movement, mechanics, deaths, target swaps, and
players who are not robots. The comparison is a **plausibility check**, never a
correction factor, and the site presents both rather than reconciling them.

## Design and accessibility notes

- Series colours come from a palette validated for colour-blind separation and contrast
  in both light and dark mode. Slots are assigned in fixed order and follow the entity,
  so filtering a chart never repaints the remaining series.
- Three light-mode slots sit below 3:1 against the light surface, so every multi-series
  chart ships direct labels at the line ends *and* a table view — identity never rests on
  colour alone.
- WoW class colours are a strong domain convention but are nowhere near colour-blind safe
  as a set of thirteen, so they appear only as a secondary cue (a dot beside a name),
  never as the series encoding.
- Dark mode is a separately chosen set of steps validated against the dark surface, not
  an automatic inversion.

## Limitations worth stating plainly

- **Sims are not logs.** Perfect play, a stationary target, no mechanics, no deaths.
- **Profiles are simc's, not yours.** Gear, talents and consumables are the tier defaults.
  Two builds being close here says little about your character specifically.
- **The talent build is fixed across the target sweep.** SimulationCraft ships one build
  per spec, and its rotations adapt to the target count while the talents do not. So the
  ten-target numbers are produced by raid single-target builds. A spec whose real
  area-damage build diverges will read low at the top of the range. Sweeping talent
  permutations per target count is a profileset problem and is on the roadmap; until then
  read the high end of the curve as "this build at ten targets", not "this spec at ten
  targets".
- **A few percent is noise.** At `--target-error 0.2`, differences under roughly half a
  percent are not real. The site shows the sampling error alongside each figure.
- **Coverage tracks simc.** Only builds with a profile for the current tier appear.
  Season 2 currently ships 26 profiles across 15 damage specs; season 1 reached 41 across
  26. The list fills in as the SimulationCraft developers work through the tier, and the
  pipeline picks up additions with no code change.

  Worth being clear about why this cannot simply be automated away: the profiles are the
  cheap part. What the SimulationCraft developers actually write is the spec module (C++
  modelling every spell interaction) and the action priority list (the rotation, hand-
  tuned by class theorycrafters). Neither can be generated from the game client. This
  project runs their simulator; it cannot replace their modelling. What *is* automatable
  on top — and worth doing — is searching talent permutations within a hero tree, which
  is a profileset sweep rather than new modelling.
- **simc's own caveats are surfaced**, per spec, on the detail view — the places where the
  simulation approximates rather than reproduces the game, straight from the people who
  wrote the module.

## Planned

**Funnel ceiling.** The current number is funnel gain under the standard rotation. Running
a second pass with `profileset_metric=prioritydps` — which makes SimulationCraft optimise
for priority-target damage instead of total damage — would give the ceiling a player
deliberately funnelling could reach. The gap between the two is arguably the more
interesting number.

**Patch-over-patch comparison.** Two forms, and they are not equally easy:

- *Tier comparison* (cheap): the `MID1` profiles for season 1 are still in the
  SimulationCraft tree, so `--tier MID1` simulates season 1 gear and talents against
  today's spell data. That answers "how did the season 1 setup change under current
  balance", and it works today.
- *True historical reconstruction* (expensive): checking out SimulationCraft at a dated
  commit gives the spell data and rotations **as they were**, which is what "how did this
  patch change the spec" actually requires. Feasible — the build is scripted — but each
  point in time costs a full rebuild plus a full simulation matrix.

Either way the dataset format needs a tier axis before this can ship; right now one
dataset is one tier.

**Popular talent builds** beyond SimulationCraft's defaults, imported from published
loadout strings.

**Per-ability funnel attribution** — which specific abilities carry the concentrated
damage, rather than just how much of it there is.
